Abstract:
We present EmoWork, a multimodal, multi-label dataset designed to support emotion and stress detection in realistic interpersonal work
settings. Interpersonal work—common in occupations such as customer service—often requires workers to regulate their emotional
expressions in response to strong affective stimuli. These demands, shaped by organizational display rules, present a unique challenge
for affective computing systems, particularly in scenarios where internal emotional states diverge from observable behaviors. Despite this,
no public datasets exist that capture such dynamics of affect in naturalistic settings. To address this gap, we collected physiological,
behavioral, and self-reported data from call center workers who engaged in role-play scenarios simulating customer service interactions
with professional actors portraying dissatisfied customers. The dataset includes self-reported affective ratings, which are used as labels
for classification, synchronized recordings from three wearable devices (i.e., Polar H10, Empatica E4, and Muse S), and features
extracted from video and audio data. The EmoWork dataset advances affective computing by offering context-rich, multimodal data
grounded in realistic interpersonal work scenarios.

Background & Summary12
Affective computing focuses on creating systems that can recognize, interpret, and understand human affect (i.e., emotion and13
stress)1. These systems serve as an affect-aware foundation for human interaction-centered disciplines. Examples include14
human-Computer Interaction (HCI)2, human-Robot Interaction (HRI)3, 4, and human–AI Interaction (HAI)5 across everyday15
contexts, such as home 6, workplace7, 8, and driving 9. Among these contexts, the workplace has emerged as a particularly16
prominent application domain, as Industry 4.0 has transformed it into an environment rich in sensing technologies and driven17
by digital innovation10, 11. Within work environments, researchers have extensively studied automatic monitoring of workers’18
mental status, such as workload 12, stress13, 14, and mood15, using diverse sensors (e.g., computer logs 16, mobile17, and wearable19
sensors18) to enhance workers’ well-being and performance.20
Prior studies relied on automatic affect-detection models, whose performance depends on securing relevant datasets. To21
acquire reliable datasets in workplace settings, it is important to reflect different contextual information such as the type of22
work (e.g., construction, service work, knowledge work, and manufacturing) because affective responses are highly context-23
dependent19, 20. However, most publicly available datasets largely focused on capturing responses to standardized affective24
stimuli (e.g., watching video 21–27 or Trier Social Stress Test (TSST) 28) (Table 1). Although some studies have attempted to25
collect affective datasets in more naturalistic scenarios (e.g., knowledge worker scenario 29, interpersonal conversations30, 31),26
these efforts still fall short in a diversity of scenarios from work types. Especially, data on interpersonal workplace interactions27
(e.g., conversations between customers and agents) remain scarce in existing datasets for affective computing. Y et, such28
scenarios are not marginal as interpersonal work is central to the modern labor force.29
Despite this gap, interpersonal work—occupations that involve direct interaction with clients or coworkers—constitutes over30
half of all global employment 32 and includes large job families such as the 2.9 million U.S. customer-service representatives31
(CSR), underscoring its prevalence in today’s workforce33. These CSR roles, especially in the service sector, are emotionally32
demanding, as they frequently expose workers to strong affective stimuli while simultaneously requiring emotional suppression33
in accordance with organizational display rules 34. Such dual demands, intense emotional elicitation, and strict regulation create34
a unique challenge for affect detection because workers’ internal states often diverge from observable expressions. Therefore,35
realistic interpersonal work scenarios are essential not only for understanding these affective dynamics but also for training and36

evaluating affect-detection models that can operate under socially regulated conditions. One prominent CSR example is call37
center work35, 36, where agents regularly engage in emotionally charged conversations with dissatisfied customers. However,38
few publicly available datasets exist that authentically reflect interpersonal emotional workload. To fill this research gap, we39
built EmoWork, a context-specific multimodal dataset captured in realistic interpersonal work scenarios.40
EmoWork is a multimodal, multi-label dataset from call agents engaged in real-time voice interactions with dissatisfied41
customers. It captures four synchronized affective states (i.e., stress, suppression, arousal, and valence), physiological and42
behavioral responses. For realistic data collection in an interpersonal work setting, we employed scripted role-play scenarios43
simulating customer service interactions as frequently used in psychology literature on emotional labor 35. We designed a44
scenario of a credit-card dispute, an issue known to provoke sensitive and challenging reactions from customers. The role-play45
scenario was designed to explicitly incorporate display rule compliance, a core aspect of emotional labor. To maximize realism,46
we hired trained actors to portray difficult customers. During the simulated calls, we measured physiological and behavioral47
responses using three wearable devices (i.e., Polar H10, Muse S, Empatica E4), a headset, and a camera. Following the48
calls, participants retrospectively reported their momentary levels of arousal, valence, stress, and emotional workload (i.e.,49
suppression). Our data, collected in a realistic interpersonal work scenario with a high emotional workload, lays the groundwork50
for future research on affect modeling in such scenarios. In the following section, we describe the experimental protocol, sensor51
suite, and technical validation in detail.52
Methods53
Ethics Statement54
We obtained IRB approval for open data collection and release from our institution (KAIST IRB) and conducted the study55
according to the approved protocol (KH2022-108). Recruitment was conducted through the managers of each local call56
center, who were contacted through institutional training coordinators. Participation was entirely voluntary, and those who57
expressed willingness to join were enrolled sequentially on a first-come, first-served basis. Before participation, all participants58
received a written explanation of the study, including the types of data to be collected (audio, video, physiological data, and59
self-reported responses), the purpose of the research, and how the collected data would be used and shared for academic60
purposes. Participants provided written informed consent acknowledging their understanding and voluntary agreement to61
data collection, anonymized data sharing, and the public release of derived datasets under IRB-approved conditions. Also,62
female participants were asked whether they were pregnant, and pregnant women were excluded from the study. During data63
collection, participants could withdraw from the study at any time. After data collection, breaks were provided that incorporated64
meditation materials to relieve any negative effects on participants’ mental well-being. To protect participants’ information, all65
data were pseudo-anonymized during the data collection process. Call agents were assigned participant numbers as identifiers,66
and the actors used only fictitious personal details (e.g., names, phone numbers, and jobs) in the simulated call scenarios. All67
raw data were securely stored on a restricted-access shared drive managed by authorized researchers, and any information that68
could potentially identify individuals was strictly excluded from publication and public data release.69
Data Collection70
Participants71
Our data collection process includes two roles: customer and call agent. The customers were played by four professional actors72
from the local theater society (female: 2, male: 2). The call agents were recruited from real call agents (female: 24, male: 7)73
working in local call centers operated by six different companies. The gender distribution (77.4% female) aligns with prior74
research showing that call center work in South Korea is heavily female-dominated 37. Their educational backgrounds included75
19 with a high school diploma and 12 with a college degree or higher (mean age = 36.19 ± 11.38). Because official statistics on76
call center demographics (e.g., education level and age) are limited and often inconsistent across government and industry77
reports, these values should be regarded as indicative rather than definitive. 34 were recruited, but three dropped out before the78
data collection procedure (one was screened due to pregnancy; another due to COVID-19 infection; and the third withdrew79
voluntarily).80
Data Collection Setup81
The data collection process consisted of two phases: 1) sensor data collection during voice calls and 2) self-reported retrospective82
data collection after voice calls 35. The second phase was carried out only with call agents, because this data set was designed to83
collect data to evaluate their emotions and stress. Data collection was conducted in three rooms throughout the study, with one84
researcher assigned to each room, for a total of three researchers to assist with data collection.85

a preparation and monitoring space and a data collection space using a partition. In the preparation and monitoring space,87

Self-reported data collection We prepared a room for the call agent in the second phase, which was divided into a preparation108
space and a self-report data collection space. In the preparation space, participants completed the post-data collection surveys109
and were instructed to store their personal belongings that could distract them to ensure participants’ immersion during the110
retrospective self-reporting process. In the self-reported data collection space, participants sat in front of a laptop to collect the111
label data measured by Likert-scale (Figure 2). We used E-prime 3.0, which has been widely used in psychology 38, 39 with a112
customized interface to record participants’ self-reported affective responses during each stimulus event (i.e., while listening to113
their own calls recorded in the previous session). Participants continuously reported their affective state by moving a mouse114
cursor on an interactive response screen (i.e., a slider with an anchor or a grid image with an anchor moving as the movements115
of the user), and the program recorded the cursor position every 100 ms 35. This allowed us to capture continuous, time-varying116
self-report trajectories rather than discrete, one-time ratings. Both left- and right-handed participants used their right hand to117
operate the mouse.118
Procedure119
The overall procedure is illustrated in Figure 3, and its details are explained in the following sections.120
Onboarding We instructed call agent-role participants (i.e., call agents) to use a pre-assigned subject number in every process121
for pseudonymization before arriving at the data collection site. Before data collection, they completed a pre-survey on basic122
demographic information (i.e., sex, age, work experience, education, and dominant hand) and baseline mental well-being such123
as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-9 40, Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) 41, Positive Psychological Capital (PPC) 42, Big124
Five Inventory (BFI)43, Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSE) 44, and Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) 45. All surveys were125
administered in Korean using validated Korean translations of the original instruments (Table 3). For the BFI, we used the126
15-item Korean short version46, which was developed and validated as a psychometrically reliable adaptation of the original127
44-item scale. Participants were asked to avoid drinking alcohol, smoking, engaging in vigorous exercise, or consuming128
caffeine on the day of data collection, as these behaviors could affect their physical responses. Upon arrival at the site, a129
researcher checked whether the pre-survey was completed and verified the subject number. The participants then read the study130
instructions and completed the user consent form.131
A researcher in the designated space explained the role and provided supplementary documents. The documents included132
predefined opening and closing scripts for call agents, containing fictional information (i.e., name and call center). While the133
opening and closing scripts were predetermined, participants responded to the customer’s requirements as they normally would134
at work, without a set script. Examples were provided to guide responses in predictable conflict situations. As in a real work135
environment, they did not have any prior information about the customer or the problem. Each participant practiced their role136
until they were familiar enough to begin data collection, and were allowed to freely ask the researcher questions before data137
collection began.138
As the final step of onboarding, participants put on the wearable devices in the following order: Polar H10, Empatica139
E4, Muse S, and a headset. The researcher helped adjust the webcam angle and the headset and ensured that everything was140
functioning properly. Customer-role participants were required to demonstrate their professional acting experience (e.g., a141

Baseline measurement Three types of baseline conditions were acquired from call agents that were designed to reflect146
typical tasks of call agents, such as speaking and typing. Baseline 1 (B1) was a condition with no stimuli, so the participants sat147
comfortably and were relaxed. Baseline 2 (B2) was a condition in which participants read a neutral script in a natural speaking148
tone, unrelated to customer service work. B2 (speaking without emotional workload) was designed to compare recorded audio149
from the main experimental setting, thereby enabling a more in-depth analysis of speech data. Baseline 3 (B3) was a condition150
in which participants typed a given script. B3 (typing without emotional stress) was designed to isolate the hand movement151
patterns that arise solely from typing behavior. It has the potential to be used later to normalize or subtract typing-related152
acceleration signals from the main experimental data, thereby enabling more accurate detection of motion artifact induced153
by mental status changes, such as stress level or emotion. Each session of these baseline conditions was designed to last154
approximately two minutes.155
Main phase We simulated a representative example of an abuse complaint call in a real call center scenario, where a customer156
complained about a card company’s reward35, 47–49. Credit card customers who had not received cash rewards contacted the call157
center to raise an issue due to a misunderstanding of the complex rewards rules. Each call agent answered three calls from158
the same customer. While the problem remained the same across all calls, the way the customer expressed their complaint159
differed. Call 1 (C1) represented a neutral scenario. Customers simply explained their problems and asked for the reason behind160
the problem without using emotionally charged language. Call 2 (C2) represented an aggressive scenario involving prosodic161
stimulation (e.g., talking fast, shouting, and a threatening tone). Note that customers were instructed to avoid using negative162
words (e.g., swearing) to minimize confusion with C3. Call 3 (C3) represented an aggressive scenario involving expressive163
stimulation (e.g., demeaning and negative words, including swearing).164
We placed restrictions on the use of excessively profane expressions and provided a list of permissible expressions to165
maintain ethical standards. Note that customers were instructed to refrain from prosodic features (e.g., shouting) to minimize166
confusion with C2. We defined C1 as less emotional stimuli or basic workload condition, C2 as high emotional prosodic stimuli167
or high workload condition, and C3 as high emotional expressive stimuli or high workload condition. Each session lasted168
approximately three to four minutes. The order of the conditions was randomized as C1-C2-C3 or C1-C3-C2 to avoid order169
effects. C1 was performed first in all cases to allow for a natural flow, while the order of the two different types of aggressive170
calls (C2 and C3) was varied to prevent order effects. In each call, customers initiated and terminated the conversation while171
call agents were not allowed to control the start and end of a call, just like in natural inbound calls. Between calls, call agents172
took a break with a one-minute guided breathing video to release emotional stress caused by the previous call. After completing173
three calls, the call agents removed the wearable devices and engaged in a 10-minute guided meditation.174
Label acquisition After the meditation session, call agents moved from the sensor data collection room to the self-report data175
collection room. Following prior work on retrospective continuous self-report during stimulus playback 35, each call was played176
back in the original order. For each call, participants performed the self-report three times in a row to assess suppression, stress,177
and emotion (i.e., arousal and valence), as illustrated in Figure 2. We intentionally chose this post-interaction, playback-based178
procedure rather than soliciting ratings during or immediately after each call to preserve workflow continuity and ecological179
validity across the three consecutive interactions and avoid dual-task interference that could alter natural conversational180
dynamics. Before the assessments began, all participants completed a practice session to familiarize themselves with the181
interface and the data recording process. Then, they were asked to read the instructions containing the meaning and example of182
each label as defined in Table 2. For example, participants listened to the C1 session a total of three times, rating suppression183
during the first listening, stress during the second, and emotion during the last.184
Simulation fidelity The experiment was conducted in a lab environment designed to reproduce realistic call-center scenarios185
as closely as possible. Because the study took place under controlled laboratory conditions, participants were informed during186
the consent process about the general purpose of the study, the number of calls to be conducted, and the types of data to be187
collected. Although this awareness could potentially influence their behavioral or affective responses, we made extensive efforts188
to ensure that the interactions resembled authentic customer service situations. To enhance realism, participants were asked to189
perform their genuine professional roles, and professional actors were recruited to portray customers. Importantly, the call190
agents were not informed that the customers were professional actors or about the specific behavioral strategies the actors191
would employ. After data collection, a brief post-experiment questionnaire assessed the perceived reality of the scenario.192

We release the raw data collected from each device. All details of the collected data are described in Data Records.202
Sensor_Audio203
We prepared three separate audio recordings for ease of data usage: customer-only, worker-only, and dyadic conversation204
recordings. We used the customer-only and the worker-only records that were stored at 44,100 Hz in ‘.wav’ format. Because of205
privacy concerns, audio files are not released to the public. Instead, we extracted well-known features that are widely used in206
previous studies on emotion and stress recognition, including a deep learning-based approach 50. We used Librosa 10.0 and207
applied a window size of 25 ms with a sliding window of 10 ms 50. Before feature extraction, we applied a pre-emphasis filter208
provided by Librosa. We then extracted features, including fundamental frequency (F0), zero crossing rate (ZCR), voiced flag,209
mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), and spectral features (centroid, bandwidth, roll-off, roll-off min, contrast) 51, 52 All210
transformed data are saved in a dictionary structure and stored as a pickle file (audio.pkl). The dyadic conversation recordings211
were used for script generation to help other researchers gain a comprehensive understanding of our scenario. Because the212
participants were Korean, all conversations were recorded in Korean. Thus, we used the Naver Clova Speech-to-Text (STT)213
service, which is optimized for Korean. A researcher verified the accuracy of each transcript and manually modified the text214
in Korean. The researcher then automatically translated it into English using ChatGPT-4o, followed by manually verifying215
the translated script. To balance accessibility with linguistic fidelity, we release the original Korean utterances alongside their216
English translations. This follows established practice in non-English speech corpora that preserve source-language transcripts217
to support downstream research (e.g., cross-lingual modeling, and prosody–text analyses) 53, 54. This data was transformed into218
JavaScript Object Notation Lines (JSONL) files at the utterance level.219
Sensor_Video220
We recorded the upper body of the call agents as an ‘.mp4’ file, which was recorded at 30 frames per second (FPS). From221
the videos, we extracted features relevant to emotion and stress detection (i.e., head movements, lip and eye movements)222
from face landmarks55, 56 utilizing OpenCV and MediaPipe. We extracted Mediapipe face_detection and face_mesh functions223
(confidence = 0.7) to extract 468 landmarks on the face. From each frame, the following features were extracted based on facial224
landmarks. If a frame was not successfully read or facial landmarks could not be detected, feature extraction was not performed.225
Nevertheless, a timestamp was generated for every attempted frame regardless of detection success. An error_type column was226
introduced to indicate the outcome of each detection attempt, with values defined as follows: 0 for success, 1 for failure to read227
the frame, and 2 for failure to detect the face and landmarks. We obtained rotation and translation vectors (3 × 1 matrices)228
from detected landmark points, as well as the total translation computed from the translation vectors. We determined eye-blink229
events between frames using the Eye Aspect Ratio (EAR), calculated from six landmarks around each eye. To estimate eye230
gaze direction, we extracted data from four iris landmarks per eye with reference to the facial center. For lip corner movements,231
we computed relative positions of a landmark at the left and right corners of the mouth with respect to the facial center (i.e., a232
point of the nose tip). All extracted data were stored in a dictionary structure and saved as a pickle file (video.pkl).233

Technical Validation329
Simulation Fidelity Assessment330
We check ecological validity with evidence that the laboratory scenario resembles real call-center work using the post survey on331
simulated scenarios. After each session, agents completed a brief post-experiment questionnaire including (1) a binary realism332
item and (2) a 7-point similarity item (1 = not at all similar, 7 = very similar) regarding resemblance to real work. On the333
binary item, 29 of 31 call-center agents (93.5%) indicated that the scenarios were realistic. On the 7-point item, agents rated the334
scenarios as similar to real work (M = 5.90, SD = 1.40). These results suggest a reasonable level of simulation fidelity.335

Time-synchronization347
We collected multimodal data from multiple devices; thus, it should be ensured that multimodal streams recorded on different348
devices are time-aligned for valid cross-modal data analyses 28. Devices were synchronized to Coordinated Universal Time349
(UTC), but the time to start recording was inconsistent. Therefore, for each participant and condition, we verified that device350
streams shared a common time range, defined as the intersection of their start and end timestamps. We define the overlap ratio351
as the duration of the common time interval divided by the minimum device-specific interval within a session. In results, across352
phone-call intervals, the integrated data duration averaged 225.46 seconds (SD = 21.36; min = 95.57; max = 273.54), indicating353
consistent session lengths appropriate for multimodal alignment, with verified overlap across devices for overall sessions.354
Dataset Validation using Machine Learning Models355
As many recent data descriptors use the machine learning models and deep learning models to validate the utility of the data356
records27, 31, 59, we trained machine learning models to classify (1) workload intensity based on interaction condition and (2)357
perceived affect states from multimodal signals. Here, we aim to demonstrate the presence of learnable signals for checking358
data quality and reusability, not a benchmarking or hypothesis-testing exercise.359
We formulated two tasks. 1) Condition classification: normal conversations were labeled as low-intensity workload (0)360
and shouting/swearing as high-intensity (1); 2) Perceived affect classification: we derived binary target labels for perceived361
stress, emotional workload (i.e., suppression), arousal, and valence from self-reported ratings via participant-specific thresholds362
(per-variable mean). We used audio-derived features; E4 temperature, EDA, and ACC; Polar H10 ECG; and Muse S EEG.363
Preprocessing (normalization, filtering, feature extraction) was fitted on training folds only and applied to the held-out fold to364
avoid leakage; processed multimodal features were then integrated for modeling. Classifiers included Random Forest (RF) 60,365
eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) 61, Decision Tree (DT) 62, Support V ector Machine (SVM)63, and k-Nearest Neighbors366
(kNN)64, commonly used in affective computing 27, 28, 65. Evaluation used Leave-One-Subject-Out (LOSO; N = 31) to estimate367
subject-independent generalization.368
Summary metrics are reported in Table 7 for task 1 and Table 8 for task 2. We report the mean of AUC, accuracy, F1-score,369
Precision, and Recall with standard deviation across held-out participants. Figure 6 shows per-participant performance by370
model for task 1. RF achieved a mean AUC of 0.946 across LOSO folds, with lower values concentrated in a small subset371
of participants (IDs 25–29); a similar pattern was observed for other algorithms (Table 7). In our protocol, each participant372
interacted with one of four actors (e.g., P25–P27 with Actor 3; P28–P29 with Actor 4). Because a single actor delivered all373
conditions for a given participant, part of the between-subject variance likely reflects actor-specific delivery as well as personal374
differences. Users building general models may consider including actor identity or conducting actor-robust validation. For375
task 2, participants with single-class outcomes due to highly skewed ratings were excluded (stress: P22, P28; suppression: P28,376
P30; valence: P1).377

Handling Incomplete Audio and Video Segments385
Audio baseline. The baseline assessment before conversation was designed to last 2 min; however, two of the 31 participants386
recorded only the first 60 s. We suggest three possible handling strategies. First, users may use a standardized 60 s baseline,387
because all recordings share the same temporal anchor (start at t = 0). Second, users can just use data without a baseline and388
focus on relative changes between conditions. Lastly, the other possible strategy is excluding these two participants (strict389
subset). Users should report the selected option and any related sensitivity analyses. Video features. We provide an error_type390
flag that distinguishes no error, face-detection failure, and feature-extraction failure. By default, we suggest discarding frames391
flagged as error_type success (0) and dropping windows whose missing-data rate exceeds a defined threshold. For392

Possible Applications396
This multimodal dataset was collected in a specific work scenario involving call agents under emotional workload, requiring397
them to suppress negative emotions and express positive emotions during voice-based customer interactions. It includes a398
package of labels measured by Likert-scale (i.e., emotion, stress, and emotional workload) suited for a specific context, along399
with multimodal data readily obtainable in everyday environments. Therefore, in the call-center domain, the data can be used400
in realizing many applications, such as employee training systems with simulated customers to help new employees practice401
emotional regulation in difficult calls; operational monitoring to identify high-workload episodes during voice-only customer402
service; and just-in-time interventions that utilize the results of automatic assessments for coaching or enhancing workers’403
well-being. With appropriate domain adaptation, the dataset can inform real-time psychological state monitoring to adjust404
task difficulty in mission-critical teams training (e.g., flight or space crews 67 and military training 68) that involve scenario405
dynamics under high mental workload and voice-based communication. It may also extend to other public-facing professions406
that routinely manage emotionally demanding interactions (e.g., flight attendants and frontline public-service staff). The407
dataset can also be used to develop toolkits (e.g., Han et al. 69) for assessing and validating algorithms that classify perceived408
stress, emotions, and emotional workload. Furthermore, this dataset can be used for privacy-preserving technologies such as409
encryption on physiological signals, as well as for educational purposes (e.g., signal data preprocessing example).410
Limitations411
This dataset is limited to data collected from a single country (i.e., South Korea), which may not reflect cultural diversity in412
emotional responses (e.g., body language) that are highly correlated with culture 70, 71. The audio data for prosodic features is413
also language-dependent data 72. Therefore, caution is required when applying this dataset to the groups that have different414
cultural backgrounds. Beyond the cultural aspect, the sample size is relatively small (N = 31), and the sex distribution is415
imbalanced (7 males). Although such an imbalance is common in the South Korean call-center industry 37, it may still restrict the416
generalizability of analyses even within the same cultural context, given the potential influence of sex differences in emotional417
labor73. Other demographic characteristics (e.g., age, career, and organization type) are also limited in diversity, which further418
constrains the dataset’s representativeness of the broader call center workforce. Separately, issues of data accessibility and419
completeness should also be noted. Specifically, the public release contains only extracted features from audio and video data420
without raw recordings, which may limit the reusability of the data for studies requiring access to the original audio signals.421
Within the audio modality, two participants’ baseline audio segments were shorter than planned (60 s instead of 2 min). Within422
the video modality, some features are incomplete due to occasional frame or face detection failures because of challenges423
inherent to naturalistic recording environments (e.g., reflections from glasses, facial occlusion, or movement). For audio and424
video features, users should consult the Usage Notes for recommended handling procedures. Finally, in the technical validation,425
the label encoding used in technical validation focused on balancing label distribution, without considering the meaning of426
arousal and valence itself. Future studies may incorporate these meanings in accordance with their specific objectives.427

guided images to answer each question. We used an image of a slider with an anchor value ranging from 1 to 20 (20-Point644
Likert-scale) that moved with the cursor for suppression and stress labeling, and utilized an image of a two-dimensional space,645
which represents arousal and valence along each axis with values ranging from 1 to 9 (9-Point Likert-scale) for emotion labeling.646
